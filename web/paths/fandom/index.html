Out there

Something to look forward to.

For the ones whose favourite people are on a screen in Seoul.

The devotion you've got is real, and it's yours to bring home. This is for the ones who poured everything into idols, comebacks, dramas and a whole alphabet, and who've been laughed at for it. Nobody here laughs. The want underneath, to belong somewhere, to see something beautiful, to be seen back, is worth taking seriously. It can point at a life with your name on it.

Keep the fandom. Bring some of the love home.

The March comeback, the year Mum was ill

The year my mother was ill, a comeback dropped in March. I had the choreography down by the weekend. The group didn't save my life, but they held a door open on the days I couldn't, and that's worth writing down plainly. When people laugh at how much it meant, they're laughing at the wrong thing; it meant that much because I needed that much. What I did with it: I kept the routine that got me through, the 6 a.m. stream, the fan chat, the album on the drive to the hospital, and I added one thing to it. My sister, on the drive back. Mum's home now. The album's still in the car.

Forty thousand people and a car park

The lights come up and forty thousand people walk out into a car park, and for two hours you were completely inside something. Then it's a hotel room, sore feet and a lightstick on the bedside table, and it's very quiet. Noted: that ache isn't embarrassing. It's what it feels like to want a life as full as the one you just borrowed, and it's a fairly accurate map. So I followed it. Next morning I messaged the two people I'd stood next to in the queue, the ones from my own city, and we've had dinner four times since. Nothing about it would make a fancam. It's the first thing on my calendar that isn't a release date.

A whole alphabet, learned for someone in Seoul

Learned a whole alphabet for someone in Seoul who doesn't know I exist. Hundreds of hours of flashcards, subtitles off, whole songs understood line by line. Not silly; that's real work, done for love. And the thing about a language is it doesn't care why you learned it. It'll talk to anyone. So I pointed it at someone who could answer back: a Tuesday-night language exchange above a café, six of us, half of them there for the same reason as me and pretending otherwise. First week I ordered coffee in it and nobody laughed. Twelfth week a woman my age asked if I wanted to get the bus back together. The flashcards are still on my phone. The bus is the better part.

What a fan can already do

Take stock of what you can already do. You memorise dates, lyrics, names, positions and release schedules. You're up at 4 a.m. for a stream, on time, every time. You defend people who'll never hear you do it. That's showing up whether you feel like it or not, and loyalty, the two things most people say they can't find. You've got them; they're just aimed at one place. What I did: I took the same calendar I use for comebacks and put one ordinary thing on it a week. A walk with a friend. A class. A meal cooked for somebody. Same colour-coding, same alarms, same seriousness. Nine months on, the calendar is half Seoul and half my own street, and both halves get me out of bed.

The parts the drama cuts

A good drama gives you a life with the boring parts cut out: the confession, the reunion, the scene in the rain. Loving that is fine. Living is different; it's mostly the cut parts. The commute, the awkward third meeting, the text that gets a slow reply. Nobody edits it. I tested this. Went on the awkward third meeting, the one the drama would skip to get to the rain. It was awkward. We talked about parking. Then the fourth one, which nobody films either, and she brought me a cutting from a plant because I'd mentioned mine had died. No rain, no soundtrack, and it answers back, which the screen never has. The ending isn't already written. I'd got so used to knowing the ending that I'd forgotten that's the good part.

A quiet word from someone who kept the photocards

You don't have to leave. Keep the group, the photocards, the friends you made in the comment section, the lightstick on the shelf. You found something you'd give up a lot for, and knowing what beauty is worth is not a flaw. Just notice where your treasure is, because the rest of you follows it there. I kept all of mine and brought a little of the devotion home: a person in the room, a meal cooked for someone, a thing made instead of watched. The fandom taught me how to look forward to something. I use it now for a Thursday dinner and a friend's birthday as much as a release date. You're worth the same search you give them.

What actually works

You keep the fandom. You just point a little of the devotion at your own street. Pick one this week.

  1. Learn ten phrases for a real conversation.Not lyrics. Ordering food, asking how someone's day was, saying you're nervous.
  2. Cook one dish for someone.Kimchi fried rice, tteokbokki, anything. Serve it to a person at your table.
  3. Join one thing that meets in a room.A language exchange, a dance class, a fan meetup that happens in an actual building.
  4. Make one thing instead of watching one.A cover, an edit, a drawing, a translation, a recipe. Post it or don't.
  5. Message one friend from before the fandom.Someone who knew you first. Ask about them before you say anything about you.

The code

  • Keep the fandom. Bring some of the love home.
  • Loving something that much is not a flaw.
  • A language doesn't care why you learned it. Point it at someone who can answer back.
  • Showing up at 4 a.m. for a stream is showing up. Use it on a Tuesday too.
  • The boring parts are the parts that answer back.
  • Getting your hopes up on purpose is allowed. A little delulu goes a long way.
  • You were made on purpose. Look forward to that too.
