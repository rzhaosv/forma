No. 12 · Your people

Getting a handle on it.

For the habit you would not explain to anyone, and the clean days after.

Everyone has a thing they’d rather not explain. For some it’s a screen after half nine, a phone in the glove box. This page is not about what’s on it. It’s about getting a handle on it the way you’d get a handle on anything: a routine, a few rules, one person who knows. Wanting to be known was never the fault. It’s the best thing about you.

The door was never locked. Clean days count, and so do the ones after a slip.

From someone whose evenings had a shut door

We eat at seven. She goes up to read around half nine, and that’s when the hour I don’t talk about used to start. Door shut, sound off, an hour that became two. We hadn’t had a real conversation since spring. Not a row. A long, polite quiet. What I started to notice was the ten minutes before. It was never the screen I wanted. It was the dishwasher running and the house going still and the feeling of standing outside a lit window. The screen was the one door that always opened. Three weeks ago I went upstairs at half nine instead. I didn’t know what to say, so I said that. She put the book down. Best evening in months. I’ve kept it up. Wednesday was the first night I didn’t have to decide.

From someone who counts the clean days

I had an app that counted days. Thirty was the record. On day thirty-one I slipped, the counter went to zero, and I sat on the kitchen floor feeling like thirty days had never happened. That was the wrong sum. A mate who’d given up smoking put it plainly: the thirty days happened, you don’t hand them back. So I binned the counter and put a calendar on the fridge. A tick for a clean day, a dot for a slip, biro, nothing clever. Dots don’t wipe ticks. The month I’m looking at has twenty-six ticks and two dots. A year ago it would have been a zero and a bad week. I’m twenty-six, I still want what everyone wants, and the tally says I’m getting a handle on it. Next month I’m going for twenty-eight.

From someone with a second phone

There’s a phone in the glove box of my car. My wife doesn’t know about it. The kids don’t. I’m not going to describe what’s on it, because that makes it sound more interesting than it is. What I’ll tell you is the cost. It costs the first thirty seconds of every time I walk through my front door, because I have to check my face before I can say hello. It costs the evenings, which used to be ours and are now mine, in a car park with the engine off. I told myself for two years nobody was being hurt. Then I noticed I hadn’t been fully in a room with my wife since the phone appeared. That’s two people. I’ve told one friend it exists. It comes out of the car on Saturday. He’s coming with me.

From someone whose phone sleeps on the landing

Nothing clever. The phone charges on the landing from ten o’clock, and there’s a nine-pound alarm clock by the bed that does one thing. The first week I lay there like I’d forgotten how to be in a room with nothing in my hand. The second week I read forty pages of a book I’d owned for three years. The third week I slept, properly, the kind where the alarm is a surprise. Here’s what I noticed. The habit had never been about the thing. It was about the phone being the last door open in the house at midnight. Move the door and the pull turned down on its own, not off, down. A good month used to be four clean days. Now a bad month is four slips. The clock’s still there. So’s the book.

From someone who said it to one person

Twenty-nine, and the thing nobody knew was that nobody knew me. Not the habit, that’s the small part. The bigger part was that I could go a month without anyone asking a question I had to think about. I’d decided the habit was the problem and being known was a luxury for later. Wrong way round. So I told a mate, at his kitchen table, in one sentence I’d practised in the car. Not the details. Just that there was a thing, and I was getting a handle on it, and I wanted one person to know. He said, fair enough, and asked if I was still coming to five-a-side. I’ve gone every Wednesday since. Twelve blokes know my name and my week now. The habit got quieter without me looking at it.

From someone steady

Mine was the screen, for fifteen years, and what helped wasn’t what I expected. Not willpower. I had plenty. I could go three weeks on willpower and then lose a weekend, and every time I decided I hadn’t gritted my teeth hard enough. Wrong diagnosis. What changed was that I told one person. Not a group, not a confession, not an app. One friend, in his kitchen, in one sentence. He said, right, and put the kettle on. Nothing was different afterwards except that one person knew, and it turned out that was what I’d been reaching for the screen to avoid feeling: that nobody did. The wanting didn’t vanish. It stopped being the loudest thing in the house. Married six years now. She knows the whole story. Looked at less, known more. I wouldn’t go back.

What actually works

None of these need gritted teeth. All of them are routine, and routine beats willpower over a year. Pick one this week.

  1. Write down the hour it happens, and what you were feeling ten minutes before.Not the thing itself. The time, and what arrived just before: flat, tired, on your own in a full house. That feeling is the actual request. The screen is only where it went.
  2. Phone on the landing from ten. A cheap alarm clock by the bed.Nothing clever. The habit lives in the last door open at midnight. Move the door and see what the pull does on its own.
  3. Keep a tally you can’t reset.A calendar on the fridge, a tick for a clean day, a dot for a slip. Dots don’t wipe ticks. Thirty days happened whether day thirty-one went well or not.
  4. Tell one person, once, without the word “sorry”.Not the details. “There’s a thing, I’m getting a handle on it, I wanted you to know.” You’re not confessing. You’re being known, which is what you were after in the first place.
  5. One evening a week with the phone in another room and a person in the same one.A partner, a flatmate, a mate on the sofa, a parent at the table. It doesn’t have to be a good conversation. It has to be a real room with you in it.

The code

  • The hunger is good. Point it at something that can feed you.
  • Willpower is a sprint. A routine is a road.
  • A slip is a dot on the calendar, not a wipe.
  • The phone sleeps on the landing.
  • Tell one person. It gets smaller once it’s shared.
  • Nobody who matters is counting.
  • You were worth knowing before you sorted any of it.

What it was actually after

  • Someone who knows your week.
  • A front door you don’t have to check your face before.
  • Not being on your own at eleven at night.
  • Being picked on purpose.
  • Mattering to one person more than to a crowd.
  • Somewhere you’re a regular.
  • To be known, and for them to stay.
