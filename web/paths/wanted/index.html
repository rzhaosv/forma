A porn habit that runs the evenings, hookups that leave you empty, burnout behind the ring light, only feeling wanted in the dark

You wanted to be wanted. That was never the problem.

For the ones whose wanting has become the thing they cannot say out loud. The screen that runs the evenings. The weekends that end emptier than they started. The ring light, the second phone, the twenty-nine years without being held. Wanting to be wanted is not the fault here. This is a page about the difference between being looked at and being known, and about a way back that does not start with gritted teeth.

From someone whose evenings belong to a screen

We eat at seven. She goes up to read around half nine, and that is when the evening I do not talk about begins. Door shut, sound off, an hour that turns into two. I tell myself it is a way to switch off. What it actually does is switch me off, so that by the time I get into bed beside her there is nothing left in me that could have been offered. We have not had a real conversation since the spring. Not a fight. Just a long, polite quiet, the kind you can keep up for years if nobody names it. The thing I have started to notice is the ten minutes before. It is never desire, exactly. It is the dishwasher running and the house going still and a feeling like standing outside a lit window. I reach for the screen because it is the one door that always opens. Last week I went upstairs at half nine instead. I did not know what to say, so I said that. She put the book down. That was the whole evening. It was the best one in months.

From someone who is good at this

I am good at it. I want to say that plainly, because people assume the ones who sell content are being taken advantage of, and some are, but I run this like a business and it pays for a life my degree never would have. I know my numbers. I know which angle of the lamp makes the messages come faster. What I did not plan for is what happens when the ring light goes off. I sit on the edge of the bed and I cannot feel my own face. Four thousand people told me today that I am wanted. I could not tell you the name of one person who knows what I am afraid of. My sister called on Sunday and I let it go to voicemail, because I did not have a version of me ready that was not for sale. That is the part that scares me. Not the work. The fact that I have been looked at so much that being seen, actually seen, by someone who is not paying, has started to feel like the dangerous thing.

From someone who never stays

I have a rule, and I did not choose it, I just noticed it one day: I do not sleep over. Ever. Friday or Saturday there is a bar, a conversation that goes well, a walk somewhere, and then at some point in the small hours I am finding my shoes in the dark and taking the stairs instead of the lift so I do not have to stand still. By Sunday afternoon I feel scraped out, and by Thursday I have forgotten that, and it goes round again. People think this life is about wanting too much. I think, for me, it is the opposite. I want so much that I cannot risk the morning, the part where someone sees your actual face in daylight and makes coffee and asks a real question. I know how to be wanted for six hours. I have no idea how to be known for a weekend. Last month someone asked me to stay and I said I had an early start, and she said, gently, that I did not have to lie. I am still thinking about that.

From someone who has never been touched

I am twenty-nine and nobody has ever held my hand on purpose. I have said that sentence out loud exactly once, to a counsellor, and I watched her not flinch, and I nearly cried from the not-flinching. What I am ashamed of is not that it has not happened. It is how loud the wanting is. How much of my day it takes up. How I can be in a meeting and the thought of someone simply sitting close to me on a sofa can arrive like weather and take the whole afternoon. I used to think the volume of it meant something was wrong with me. That a normal person would want this quietly, or not at all. What the counsellor said, in the end, was that the wanting was loud because it had been waiting a long time, and that waiting things get loud, and that this was not a character flaw, it was a queue. I do not know what happens next. I have started sitting in the middle of the row at the cinema instead of the end. That is as far as I have got. It counts.

From someone with a second phone

There is a phone in the glove box of my car. My wife does not know about it. Our children do not know about it. I am not going to describe what is on it, because you already know, and because describing it is a way of making it sound more interesting than it is. What I will tell you is the cost, since nobody does. It costs me the first thirty seconds of every time I walk through my own front door, because I have to check my face before I can say hello. It costs me the ability to look at my daughter when she talks about trust. It costs me the evenings, which used to be ours and are now mine, spent in a car park with the engine off. I told myself for two years that nobody was being hurt. Then I noticed that I had not been fully in a room with my wife since the phone appeared. That is somebody being hurt. It is two people. I have not thrown it away yet. I have told one friend it exists. That is the first true sentence I have said about it, and it is harder to keep something in a glove box once one person knows it is there.

From someone steady

Mine was the screen, for the better part of fifteen years, and I want to tell you what actually helped, because it was not what I expected. It was not willpower. I had plenty of that. I could go three weeks on willpower and then lose a whole weekend, and every time I did, I decided the problem was that I had not gritted my teeth hard enough. That was the wrong diagnosis. What changed was that I told one person. Not a group, not a confession, not an app that counts days. One friend, in his kitchen, in a sentence I had practised in the car. He said, all right, and asked if I wanted tea. Nothing about me was different after that except that one person knew, and it turned out that was the thing I had been reaching for the screen to avoid feeling: that nobody knew me. Once someone did, the wanting did not disappear. It just stopped being the loudest thing in the house. I have been married six years now. She knows the whole story. I am looked at less than I used to be, and known more, and I would not go back for anything.

Small things that are actually yours to do

None of these need gritted teeth. All of them move the wanting a little closer to what it was for. Pick one this week.

  1. Name the hour it happens, and what you were feeling ten minutes before.Write it down. Not the thing itself. The time, and the feeling that arrived just before: bored, unseen, tired, alone in a full house. That feeling is the real request. The screen is only where it went.
  2. One full evening a week with the phone in another room and a person in the same one.A partner, a flatmate, a friend on the sofa, a parent at the table. It does not have to be a good conversation. It has to be a real room with a real person in it, and you in it too.
  3. Tell one safe person the true sentence, once, without the word “sorry”.One sentence. “This is happening, and I do not want it to.” No apology, no backstory, no plan. You are not confessing. You are being known, and that is the thing you were actually after.
  4. A week where the screen stays off, and you notice what the wanting is actually asking for.Not a test you can fail. An experiment. When it gets loud, ask it what it wants. The answer is almost always on the list below, and it is never a screen.
  5. One date, or one conversation, where the goal is to be known and nothing else.Lights on, phone away, one question you have never asked out loud. No outcome. The evening is a success if one true thing about you got said and somebody stayed.

Things worth believing on the way

  • Wanting to be wanted is not the problem. It never was.
  • Desire was made for closeness. It is not wrong for wanting that.
  • You can be looked at by thousands and known by no one.
  • A body is not for sale, and it is not for hiding.
  • The hour you reach for the screen is the hour you are loneliest. Start there.
  • Nobody who matters is counting.
  • The way back does not start with willpower. It starts with one person who knows.

What the wanting is actually asking for

  • To be held.
  • To be chosen on purpose.
  • To be seen with the lights on.
  • To be safe enough to be soft.
  • To not be alone at eleven at night.
  • To matter to one person more than to a crowd.
  • To be known, and for them to stay.
