Back on your feet

Making it right, slowly.

For anyone who did the thing and wants the next decade to say otherwise.

You did it; that part is settled, and nobody here needs the details. This is for anyone who wants the next ten years to say something different, and knows that takes more than hoping it blows over. A plain way of making it right: the four-sentence apology, the standing order, the same door on the same Tuesday. You were never as far gone as the record says.

The road back is ordinary. It starts with one phone call.

From someone who made the call

I owed an apology that was eleven years old. I had rehearsed it so many times it had become a speech, and speeches are for the speaker. So I cut it down to four sentences: what I did, that it was wrong, that I am sorry, and nothing about my reasons. I rang on a Tuesday at seven because that is when I knew he would be washing up. He was quiet for a bit. Then he said he had wondered whether I would ever call. That was the whole conversation, four minutes. I do not know if we are friends. I know the apology is no longer mine to carry, and I know my Tuesdays feel different. Next is his sister, who is owed the same.

From someone who paid it back

I took money that was not mine. Not a fortune; enough. For years I told myself I would pay it back when I could, which is a sentence that means never. What worked was making it boring. Standing order, forty pounds a month, first of the month, straight to her account, with a one-line note the first time and nothing after. She did not reply, and she did not need to. Thirty-one months so far, nineteen to go. What I noticed is that the number went down every month whether I felt sorry or not, and feeling sorry had never moved it an inch. Doing it moved it. I keep the spreadsheet on the fridge. It is the most honest thing in the house.

From someone who stopped rehearsing

I walked home rehearsing. Every version of the speech had reasons in it, and every version made me sound slightly better than I had been. My mum saw me from the end of the road and came out to meet me before I got to the gate. She did not ask for the speech. She asked if I had eaten. I had been so certain the door was bolted that I had spent two years standing outside it, telling myself it was, because that was easier than knocking. I am not saying it was simple after. There were hard conversations, and there should have been. But the hard part was never the door. It was the walk, and I could have taken it two years sooner.

From someone who kept a record

I kept a record against myself. Every date, every thing I said, the night I was not there. I was my own prosecutor and I was good at the job; I had years of practice. Here is what I noticed when I finally showed the list to someone: nobody else had one. Nobody remembered the thing I said in 2019 the way I did. The case was never as strong as I had made it, and I was the only one still arguing it. So I quit that job. Not by pretending it did not happen, but by putting the list in a drawer and doing the next right thing instead of re-reading the last wrong one. The drawer is still there. I open it less. It is not where I live.

From someone who went back to the same town

I did not move away, which everyone said I should. I went back to the same shop, the same pub, the same pitch on Sunday morning, and I let people look. The first month was hard, and it should have been. What I did was keep turning up, on time, sober, with nothing to say for myself except hello. I coached the under-nines because they needed someone and nobody else would. Around month four somebody asked me to hold their coat, which sounds like nothing and was not nothing. Reputations are made of Tuesdays, not speeches. I am nineteen months in. The story people tell about me is starting to have a second half, and I did not write it; I just showed up for it.

From someone steady

The record you keep against yourself is longer than anyone else's, and you are the only one still reading it. Wanting your name back is a fair thing to want. Aim it at the right thing: the day, not the verdict. Make the call. Pay the forty pounds. Turn up at the same door on the same Tuesday until turning up is boring. Turning around is allowed at any age, from any distance, after any number of goes; sixty is not too old and the fourth attempt is not too many. When a debt is settled properly it is not filed somewhere to be brought up later; it is gone. You are allowed to stop carrying the receipt. The road back is ordinary. Walk it.

What actually works

Five things. Small, specific, and none of them a speech.

  1. Write the apology in four sentences.What you did, that it was wrong, that you are sorry, and nothing about your reasons. Then send it, or say it.
  2. Set up the standing order.Whatever you owe, in money or in time, make the repayment boring and automatic. Forty pounds on the first beats a grand someday.
  3. Go back to the same door.Same shop, same pitch, same Tuesday. Let people look. Turn up until turning up is nothing.
  4. Do the next right thing, not the last wrong one.When the record starts playing, pick one small act for today and do that instead of re-reading.
  5. Tell one person the whole of it.One, not everyone. Someone steady, who will not flinch and will not let you shrink it either.

The code

  • That part is settled. Today is not.
  • Four sentences, no reasons.
  • Pay it back boringly. Forty pounds on the first.
  • Reputations are made of Tuesdays, not speeches.
  • Do the next right thing, not the last wrong one.
  • Nobody else is still reading your record.
  • The door was never locked. Knock.

You were never as far gone as the record says. Come home.
