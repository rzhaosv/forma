A path · More than the night

For the ones who come alive when the drop hits.

The night is good, and you are there for more than the night. This is for people who know exactly what the build feels like in the chest: the wristband, the group chat planning since January, the stranger who hugged you at the rail, the sunrise set you will describe for years. And the Tuesday after, with the lights on. Nobody here will tell you to stop going. This is about keeping what the night gives you.

The feeling at the build is real. It can last past Sunday.

From someone who plans the year around it

There are three dates on my wall. EDC in May, Tomorrowland in July, and a smaller one in the desert in October that I will not name because it is still small. Everything else is the space between them. I count down in the group chat, on a widget on my phone, in the shower. I know the exact feeling I am counting toward: the Ultra mainstage at eleven at night, Anyma’s visuals two storeys tall, the build climbing until my ribs are humming, forty thousand people holding one breath, and then the drop, and the lasers open like a ceiling coming off. For about four seconds I am not a person with a lease and a manager. I am just there. Here is the honest part. I have started to notice that the countdown is the real drug. The nine weeks before are better than the fourth day after. I used to think I was counting toward the drop. I think I am counting toward the version of me who is not waiting for anything. I would like to meet him on a Wednesday.

From someone who is tired of being looked at

This will sound ungrateful. I am good looking. I have known since I was fifteen, and in this scene it is a currency, and I have spent it. The mesh top, the glitter on the collarbones, the photo at the rail that has four hundred likes before the set is over. People look at me and I can feel it land: a hand on my back that I did not ask for and did not mind, a drink that appears. I used to love it. Some nights I still do. But at a Fred again.. set last summer, the one where he plays the voice notes over the piano and the whole crowd goes soft, a guy I had been dancing with for an hour turned to me at the peak and said, you are so hot. That was the thing he had to say at the most beautiful moment of the night. And I thought: he does not know what I do, what I am afraid of, my mother’s name. He is looking straight at me and he cannot see me. I want to be wanted. I am not going to pretend otherwise. I would just like, once, to be wanted by someone who knows what he is wanting. There is a guy in my Wednesday group who asks about my week before anything else. I have started answering.

From someone who met her people at the rail

I went to my first festival alone. Everyone I asked bailed, I had already bought the ticket, so I drove six hours and put my tent between two groups who were clearly not going to talk to me. Day two, Charlotte de Witte, second row, I had held my spot for three hours with a warm water bottle and no plan. The girl next to me had kandi up to both elbows. She looked at my empty wrists and did not say anything, just took one off and did the thing with the hands, peace, love, unity, respect, and slid it on me. Her boyfriend gave me his last electrolyte packet. When the set ended and the crowd surged we grabbed each other’s hands so nobody got lost. That was seven years ago. She was at my father’s funeral in March. He was there too, and he brought the food. PLUR was something I had seen on a t-shirt. That night it meant: we noticed you were alone and we decided you would not be. I have tried to do that for one person at every set since. It is the only ritual I keep.

From someone who goes home alone

Every weekend I go looking for the same thing and every weekend I find the wrong version of it. The eye contact across the floor. You know the one. The bass is doing something to your spine, the strobe turns everything into a series of photographs, and in one of them there is a girl looking right at you and she does not look away. Or a guy with that jawline and the sleeves cut off, who nods once. It is the best feeling I know, the second before either of you moves. I have gotten good at what comes after. The number, the car, the morning where one of us is polite. And then Sunday, and I am on my couch, and I have the number and not the person. I have never once been lonely on the floor. I am lonely every single time I leave it. What I have finally admitted, and only here, is that I am not chasing her. I am chasing the four seconds where somebody looked at me like I was the only thing in the room. I would like that to last longer than a set. I think it can. I think it starts with asking a name first.

From someone on the Tuesday after

It is Tuesday. The wristband is still on. I wore it into the standup on Monday and my manager looked at it and looked away. I am not taking it off yet. Three days ago I was at a Rüfüs sunrise set with my arms around two people I had met the night before, and the sky went pink behind the stage, and I cried, actually cried, and nobody thought it was strange. Today I ate cereal for dinner and could not answer a text. I am not going to lie about the chemistry part. I took things, not a lot, and the bill for that comes due on about day three, and today is day three. Everything is grey and flat and I keep telling myself it is just brain chemistry, which is true and does not help. What helps a little: I know it passes. What helps more: my rail friend messaged this morning, not about the next one, just, how are you doing, drink water. I am going to sleep tonight instead of watching the aftermovie. The wristband comes off Thursday. I have decided that on purpose.

From someone steady, still going at 34

I am thirty-four and I still go. Fewer, and differently. I used to do four days on a campsite; now it is one night, a Berlin basement that opens at midnight and is still going at six when I walk out into daylight with my ears ringing and a coffee from the bakery that opens for exactly us. Fisher at the club, hands in the air, the whole room grinning like idiots. What changed is not that I love it less. It is that I stopped needing it to do a job it cannot do. In my twenties every set was an audition. Am I attractive enough, am I with the right group, did the right person see me. I was checking, constantly, and you cannot dance while you are checking. Now I go with the same three people. We know each other’s bad years. We leave when someone is done. The drop still hits. The build still lifts my chest the way it did at twenty-two. It is just that when I get home, someone is there, and she knows what the night was for, and she is not on the guest list and never wanted to be.

Small things to try between the nights

None of these ask you to stop going. All of them are about keeping what the night gives you. Pick one this week.

  1. One set, all the way through, alone, lights off.The one you would put in your obituary. No phone, no aftermovie, start to finish. Notice what happens in your chest at the build. That is the thing you are there for.
  2. Text the rail friend on a Wednesday.Not about the next one. No lineup, no link. Just ask how they are, and see what comes back when there is nothing to plan.
  3. Ask a name before a number.One conversation this weekend with someone whose face stopped you. Their name, one true thing about their week. Then decide about the number.
  4. A sober set, once.Just to see if the feeling is still there. It is. And now you will know it was yours.
  5. Sleep the full night after, before the group chat.The aftermovie will keep. The chat will still be there at noon. Give your body the night back before you hand the weekend to your phone.

Things worth believing on the way

  • The feeling in your chest at the build is real. It is telling you what you want.
  • Being wanted is nice. Being known is what you are actually looking for.
  • Your body is good. It is also not the whole of you.
  • The best night of your life should not be the only one.
  • Nobody at the rail is keeping score of your face.
  • The hug from a stranger counts. So does the one from a friend on a Tuesday.
  • You are allowed to come down. You are allowed to want more than the night.

In fairness to the night

Things the night gets right.

Nobody is asking you to stop. The floor teaches things a quiet room cannot. Keep these.

  • Strangers can be kind for no reason. Nobody is paying them to.
  • A crowd can move as one, and nobody has to be in charge of it.
  • Your body knows how to feel joy. You did not have to learn it.
  • You are more beautiful when you are not checking. So is everybody.
  • The sunrise set is better with someone who will remember it with you.
  • Four seconds of being seen is real. It is also a preview.
  • The silence after the last set is not empty. It is where the rest of your life is waiting.
