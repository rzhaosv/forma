Why do I love mafia movies, GTA gamer adult life, gaming feels like the only place I matter, loyalty and respect

For the ones who wanted a crew, a code and a name.

For the ones who know the back room behind the ramen shop and the long table where the word family gets said. Who can quote what Michael tells Sonny, and run a guild better than their manager runs a team, and feel more like somebody in Kamurocho at two in the morning than in any room they walk into by day. Nobody here will call it a waste of time. We take it seriously enough to ask what it is for.

From someone who has seen every one of them

Sunday night, lights off, the Corleone wedding on the screen for a number of times I stopped counting years ago. I know it the way other people know the house they grew up in. The oranges on the table before somebody dies. Luca Brasi practising his speech in the corner. Michael in the uniform telling Kay, 'That's my family, Kay. It's not me.' I say the lines under my breath and I do not apologise for it. People assume it is the guns. It is not the guns. It is the study with the blinds half shut, the men who come in to ask for something, and the man behind the desk who says he will take care of it, and does. I work somewhere a reorganisation gets announced by email and your manager finds out the same morning you do. Nobody there would cross the street for me. So I sit in the dark with men who would, and I try not to watch the ending. Sonny at the toll booth. Fredo in the boat with the rosary. Michael alone in the garden with nobody left to say the word family to. I have seen that ending a hundred times. I am starting to think it was always the point.

From someone who runs a raid group

Tuesday, 8:58 p.m. Forty-one green dots in the voice channel, three continents, a raid schedule that has held for six years. Somebody's baby crying in the background in Perth. 'Pull in two,' I say, and forty people I have never shaken hands with go quiet and do exactly what I tell them. I keep the spreadsheet. I wrote the code of conduct. I handled the man who emptied the guild bank, and I have talked a nineteen-year-old through his parents' divorce at two in the morning with my mic on push-to-talk so he would not hear that I was crying too. I have never once been late. At work I am the guy who forgets his timesheet and gets the email about it. Then last spring a woman from Manchester flew across an ocean to stand in a church in Ohio for a man she had only known as a dwarf paladin, and I stood there in a rented suit and understood. The organisation is real. The trust is real. The only thing that is not real is the dragon. What I cannot answer yet is why I only show up like that for people I have never met, in a city that does not exist, and what it would cost to do it in the room I actually live in.

From someone who got close to the real thing

No names, no city. Just a back room behind a restaurant, a card table with a cigarette burn in the felt, and an envelope put in my hand at twenty-two that made me feel, for four seconds, like the most important man alive. I drove. I waited outside. I kept my mouth shut and was proud of it, because I had seen Goodfellas and I knew the two rules. Then an older man, one of the ones who lasted, sat me down over a coffee and said the thing the films only say sideways. 'They love you until you cost them something. That's the whole code, kid. The rest is decoration.' The loyalty runs one way, upward. The men at the bottom who mean it, who would actually do the time, get used up first, precisely because they mean it. I watched it happen to my friend. He kept every promise he ever made and it bought him a funeral in the rain with eleven people at it. I still watch the films, the way a man who quit smoking stands near the door. What I wanted is what you want: somebody at my back. I have it now. A brother-in-law, a foreman, a card game that has lasted eleven years. None of them will ever hand me an envelope.

From someone who games until three

Kamurocho, two in the morning. Neon on wet pavement, the Millennium Tower over everything, a kid in a leopard-print shirt looking for trouble outside the convenience store. I know those streets better than my own town. I tell people I am a night owl. The truth is the console is the only thing that turns my head off. Between eleven and three there is a mission, a map, and a man in a grey suit who keeps his word whatever it costs him, and I am good at it, and nothing needs me to answer for anything. Kiryu did ten years for a killing that was not his, to protect his brother. I cannot return a text. Then four hours of sleep and hating myself at the desk. The problem is not screen time. The problem is that the quiet hours are when the other stuff comes in: the job, the friends I have not called, the sense that I am thirty-one and nobody is keeping count of me. The game keeps count. I tried throwing it out; that lasted nine days. Now, one night a week, it goes off at midnight and I lie there and let the stuff come in and write down one line of it. It is worse than the game. It is the only thing that has ever made the mornings better.

From someone whose dad played it on repeat

My whole childhood, every few weeks: the trumpet, the undertaker in the dark, 'I believe in America.' My father never bothered with the sequels. He put the first one on after dinner and we did not talk, and I learned to read the room from the credits. I hated it for years, the way you hate anything that gets more of your parent than you do. He died in March. In his things I found the case worn white at the corners, and I put it on and watched it properly for the first time as a grown man. What I saw was a man who wanted two things, for his family to be safe and for his word to mean something, and who got the second at the price of the first. My father was quiet. Forty years at a plant that did not know his name, and never once a raised voice at home. I think the film was the only place he ever saw a version of himself taken seriously: a man who looks after his people and is respected for it without shouting. He was studying it. Vito in the tomato garden with the grandson and the orange peel in his teeth. I wish I had sat beside him even once. I would have asked which scene he was waiting for.

From someone steady

I found it in a volunteer fire company, which nobody would put in a film. Half of them are over fifty and the dispatcher's name is Carol. But my first year, standing in the bay at five in the morning while a man twice my age checked my straps without a word, I understood I had everything I had loved in the films and the games. A crew. A chain of command. A code you actually have to keep. A name that means something in the four towns we cover. The sure knowledge that the person next to you will not leave you. De Niro in Heat says not to get attached to anything you cannot walk out on in thirty seconds. That is the sales pitch. Arthur Morgan rides for a man who says it is all for the family, right up until it is not. That is the ending. What I found has the loyalty and it points somewhere. It costs me Tuesday nights and a pager I cannot switch off, and nobody has to die for it. What you want exists. It is nearly always in a room that looks boring from the outside: a kitchen, a gym at six, a union hall, a trade. Find the one where the code holds and the funeral is optional.

Small things that are actually yours to do

None of these ask you to give up the games or the films. All of them move a little of what you love into a room with people in it. Pick one this week.

  1. Name what the story gives you, in one sentence.Not the plot. The feeling. A crew that keeps its word, a place where you are somebody, a rule you could live by. Write the sentence down. That is what you are actually looking for.
  2. Take one skill from the game into a room with people.If you can run a raid, you can run a meeting. If you can grind a speedrun, you can grind a certification. Pick the skill you are proudest of and use it once, this week, somewhere it has a witness.
  3. Find one crew that meets in person.A gym, a kitchen, a volunteer shift, a rec league, a trade. It will look boring from the outside. Go three times before you decide.
  4. Keep the code you admire and write it down as your own.Keep your word. Do not talk about people behind their backs. Show up. Three or four lines, on paper, yours. Then keep it in the rooms where it costs you something.
  5. One night a week, the console is off by midnight.No other rule. Not fewer hours, not a detox, not a lecture. One night, one clock, and notice what comes in when it is quiet.

Things worth believing on the way

  • Wanting a crew that keeps its word is not a flaw.
  • Loyalty is a virtue. It deserves a worthy object.
  • The real ones die for men who would not cross the street for them.
  • Respect that has to be taken was never yours.
  • The hours you put into a game were practice in showing up.
  • You can be feared or you can be trusted. Only one of them lets you sleep.
  • The name that matters is the one people say when you are not in the room.

What the stories are really about

It was never about the crime.

Every one of them is about a man who wants to matter to somebody, in a world that promises to make it happen fast. The sake cup is exchanged, the envelope is accepted, the word family is said out loud at a table, and for the length of the film there is somewhere you belong and a rule to live by. Niko Bellic steps off the boat wanting out before he is even in; Arthur Morgan keeps riding for Dutch long after he has stopped believing him; Tony Soprano sits across from Dr. Melfi and cannot say why a family of ducks made him cry. That is not a taste for crime, it is a hunger for loyalty, competence and a name, dressed in the only costume that made it look serious. The stories grip because the hunger is real, and they end in the rain because the world they are set in cannot feed it.

Things the stories get right

  • The debt is never paid off.
  • The loyal die first.
  • The boss cries at the funeral he ordered.
  • The money is never the point.
  • Everyone wants out by the last reel.
  • Omertà keeps everyone's secrets except yours.
  • The only ones who make it out have a family that is not the family.
