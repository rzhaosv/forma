No. 16 · Out there

A few people who'd actually show up.

For men who rewatch the family dinners and want one of their own.

What you're after exists: a few people who'd actually show up, a few rules you have to keep, and a name that means something. This is for the ones who know the back room behind the ramen shop and the long table where the word family gets said. Nobody here calls it a waste of time. We take it seriously enough to ask what it's for.

The crew you want exists. It meets in a boring room.

The Corleone wedding, Sunday nights

Sunday night, lights off, the Corleone wedding on again. I know it the way you know the house you grew up in: the oranges on the table, Luca Brasi practising his speech in the corner, Michael in uniform telling Kay it's his family, not him. People assume it's the guns. It was never the guns. It's the study with the blinds half shut, and the man behind the desk who says he'll take care of it and does. I work somewhere a reorganisation arrives by email. Noted: there's a man on my floor who checks on me every week and wants nothing for it. I'd been sitting in the study with the blinds shut without noticing. Last Sunday I switched the film off after the wedding and rang him instead.

Tuesday, 8:58 p.m., forty-one green dots

Tuesday, 8:58 p.m. Forty-one green dots in the voice channel, three continents, a raid schedule that's held for six years. Somebody's baby crying in the background in Perth. I say pull in two and forty people I've never shaken hands with go quiet and do exactly what I say. I keep the spreadsheet, I wrote the rules, I handled the one who emptied the guild bank. I've never once been late. At work I'm the one who forgets his timesheet. Noted: the organisation is real, the trust is real; the only thing that isn't real is the dragon. So I started finding out what it costs to do it in the room I actually live in. Rec league, two streets over, Thursdays. Fourteen weeks. Not late yet.

Close to the real thing, once

No names, no city. A back room behind a restaurant, a card table with a cigarette burn in the felt, and an envelope in my hand at twenty-two. I drove, I waited outside, I kept my mouth shut, because I'd seen Goodfellas and knew the two rules. Then an older man sat me down over a coffee and said the thing the films only say sideways: they love you until you cost them something. The loyalty runs one way, upward. The ones at the bottom who mean it get used up first, because they mean it. I watched that happen to my friend. What I wanted is what you want: somebody at my back. I have it now. A brother-in-law, a foreman, a card game that's lasted eleven years. Nobody hands me envelopes.

Kamurocho, two in the morning

Kamurocho, two in the morning. Neon on wet pavement, the Millennium Tower, a kid in a leopard-print shirt looking for trouble outside the convenience store. Between eleven and three there's a mission, a map, and a man in a grey suit who keeps his word whatever it costs him. Kiryu did ten years for a killing that wasn't his, to protect his brother; I couldn't return a text. Noted: the quiet hours are when the other stuff comes in, the job, the friends I haven't rung, and the game keeps count. Throwing the console out lasted nine days. Now, one night a week, it goes off at midnight and I write down one line of whatever comes in. Worse than the game. The only thing that's ever made the mornings better.

My dad's copy, worn white at the corners

Every few weeks, growing up: the trumpet, the undertaker in the dark, 'I believe in America.' My father never bothered with the sequels. He put the first one on after dinner and we didn't talk. He died in March. In his things I found the case worn white at the corners, and I watched it properly for the first time as a grown man. What I saw was a man who wanted two things, his family safe and his word to mean something, and got the second at the price of the first. My father was quiet. Forty years at a plant, never a raised voice at home. I think it was the only place he saw a version of himself taken seriously. This year I'm putting it on for my nephew, and I'm sitting beside him.

A quiet word from someone who found it

Found it in a volunteer fire company. Half of them are over fifty and the dispatcher's name is Carol. My first year, standing in the bay at five in the morning while a man twice my age checked my straps, I had everything I'd loved in the films and the games. A crew. A chain of command. A code you have to keep. A name that means something in four towns. De Niro in Heat says don't get attached to anything you can't walk out on in thirty seconds; that's the sales pitch. Arthur Morgan rides for a man who says it's all for the family, right up until it isn't; that's the ending. What I found has the loyalty and it points somewhere. It costs me Tuesday nights and a pager. Nobody has to die for it.

What actually works

None of these ask you to give up the games or the films. All of them move a little of what you love into a room with people in it. Pick one this week.

  1. Write down what the story gives you, in one sentence.Not the plot. A crew that keeps its word, a place where you're somebody, a rule you could live by. That sentence is what you're actually after.
  2. Take one skill from the game into a room with people.If you can run a raid, you can run a meeting. If you can grind a speedrun, you can grind a certification. Use it once this week somewhere it has a witness.
  3. Find one crew that meets in person.A gym, a kitchen, a volunteer shift, a rec league, a trade. It will look boring from the outside. Go three times before you decide.
  4. Write the code you admire down as your own.Keep your word. Don't talk about people behind their backs. Show up. Three or four lines, on paper. Then keep it in the rooms where it costs you something.
  5. One night a week, the console is off by midnight.No other rule. Not fewer hours, not a lecture. One night, one clock, and notice what comes in when it's quiet.

The code

  • Wanting a few people who'd show up is not a flaw.
  • Loyalty is a virtue. Give it a worthy object.
  • The real ones die for men who wouldn't cross the street for them.
  • Respect that has to be taken was never yours.
  • Every hour in the game was practice at showing up.
  • You can be feared or you can be trusted. Only one lets you sleep.
  • Your seat at the table was given, not earned. Sit down.

What the stories are really about

It was never about the crime.

Every one of them is about a man who wants to matter to somebody, in a world that promises to make it happen fast. The sake cup gets exchanged, the envelope gets accepted, the word family gets said out loud at a table, and for the length of the film there's somewhere you belong and a rule to live by. Niko Bellic steps off the boat wanting out before he's in; Arthur Morgan keeps riding for Dutch long after he's stopped believing him; Tony Soprano sits across from Dr. Melfi and can't say why a family of ducks got to him. That isn't a taste for crime. It's a hunger for loyalty, competence and a name, in the only costume that made it look serious. The stories grip because the hunger is real, and they end in the rain because the world they're set in can't feed it.

Things the stories get right

  • The debt is never paid off.
  • The loyal go first.
  • The boss cries at the funeral he ordered.
  • The money was never the point.
  • Everyone wants out by the last reel.
  • Omertà keeps everyone's secrets except yours.
  • The only ones who make it out have a family that isn't the family.
