Why do I love mafia movies, GTA gamer adult life, gaming feels like the only place I matter, loyalty and respect

Wanting a crew, a code and a name is not a flaw.

For the ones who know every line of the crime sagas and every street of the open-world cities. Who run a guild better than their manager runs a team. Who feel more like somebody in a game at two in the morning than in any room they walk into by day. Nobody here is going to tell you it is a waste of time. We are going to take it seriously enough to ask what it is really for.

From someone who has seen every one of them

I have watched The Godfather more times than I have watched anything, and I can tell you which scenes were shot in which order. I know the codes by heart. You do not talk to the police. You do not go against the family. You keep your word or you lose your name. People assume it is the violence I am after. It is not; I skip some of those parts now. What I keep coming back to is the dinner table. A room full of men who would take a bullet for each other, who know exactly what they owe and to whom, where nobody has to guess whether they belong. I work in an office where a reorganisation gets announced by email and your manager finds out the same morning you do. Nobody there would cross the street for me, and I would not for them, and we all know it. So I watch a story about men who would, and I try not to notice how the story ends. It ends with almost all of them dead, mostly killed by each other. I have watched that ending a hundred times. I am starting to think it is the point.

From someone who runs a raid group

I lead a guild. Forty-odd people, three continents, a raid schedule that has held for six years. I keep a spreadsheet of who can make Tuesdays. I have written a code of conduct, run recruitment, handled a theft from the guild bank, and talked a nineteen-year-old through his parents' divorce at two in the morning in a voice channel. I have never once been late. At work I am the guy who forgets to submit his timesheet. My manager would not believe any of this, and for a long time I did not think it counted, because it was a game. Then a woman from the guild flew across an ocean to be at another member's wedding, and I stood there in a rented suit and thought: this is real. The organisation is real. The trust is real. The only thing that is not real is the dragon. The question I am sitting with now is why I only show up like that for people I have never met in a place that does not exist, and what it would take to do it in the room I actually live in.

From someone who got close to the real thing

I am not going to give you details. I will say I grew up around it, that I was useful to some people for about two years in my early twenties, and that I got out because somebody older than me sat me down and said the thing nobody in the films says plainly. He said: they love you until you cost them something. That is the whole code. Everything else is decoration. The loyalty runs one way, upward, and the men at the bottom, the ones who mean it, the ones who would actually do the time and keep their mouths shut, are the ones who get used up first. Precisely because they mean it. I watched it happen to a friend. He kept every promise he made and it bought him nothing. I still like the films. I watch them the way a former smoker smells cigarettes, with something that is not quite longing. What I wanted was what you want. Somebody at my back. I have it now. It is a brother-in-law, a foreman and a guy I have played cards with for eleven years. None of them will ever ask me to do anything I cannot tell my kids about.

From someone who games until three

I tell people I am a night owl. The truth is that the console is the only thing that reliably turns my head off. Between eleven and three there is a mission, a map, a next thing, and I am good at it, and nothing in the world needs me to answer for anything. Then I get four hours of sleep and hate myself at my desk. I have read every article about screen time and I could write one. That is not the problem. The problem is that the quiet hours are when the other stuff comes in. The job I am not proud of. The friends I have not called. The sense that I am thirty-one and nobody would notice if I was not there. The game notices. It has been keeping count of everything I have ever done in it. I do not think the fix is to throw the console out. I tried that; it lasted nine days. What I have started doing is smaller. One night a week it goes off at midnight and I lie there and let the stuff come in, and I write down one line of it, and I go to sleep. It is worse than the game. It is also the only thing that has ever made the mornings better.

From someone whose dad played it on repeat

My father watched The Godfather every few weeks for my entire childhood. Not the sequels. The first one. He would put it on after dinner and we would not talk, and I learned to read the room from the credits. I hated it for years, the way you hate anything that gets more of your parent's attention than you do. He died in the spring, and going through his things I found the case worn white at the edges, and I put it on. I had never really watched it as an adult. What I saw was a story about a man who wanted, more than anything, for his family to be safe and to keep his word, and who got the second thing at the cost of the first. My father was a quiet man who worked forty years at a job that did not respect him. He never raised his voice. I think the film was the only place he ever saw a version of a man like him, a man who takes care of his people and is taken seriously for it. I think he was studying it. I wish I had sat down and watched it with him even once. I would have asked him which scene he was waiting for.

From someone steady

I found it in a volunteer fire company, which is not a place anyone would write into a story. Nobody there is glamorous. Half of them are over fifty, and the dispatcher's name is Carol. But I noticed, my first year, that it had every single thing I had loved in the games and the films: a crew, a chain of command, a code you actually have to keep, a name that means something in the four towns we cover, and the sure knowledge that the person next to you will not leave you. I have never once felt that in a job. I have felt it there every week for nine years. The difference between what I found and what the stories sell is that nobody has to die for it. The loyalty is real and it points somewhere. It costs me Tuesday nights and a pager I cannot turn off. Here is what I would say to somebody still looking. What you want exists. It is nearly always in a room that looks boring from the outside. A kitchen. A gym at six in the morning. A search-and-rescue team, a union hall, a trade. Go find one where the code holds and the funeral is optional.

Small things that are actually yours to do

None of these ask you to give up the games or the films. All of them move a little of what you love into a room with people in it. Pick one this week.

  1. Name what the story gives you, in one sentence.Not the plot. The feeling. A crew that keeps its word, a place where you are somebody, a rule you could live by. Write the sentence down. That is what you are actually looking for.
  2. Take one skill from the game into a room with people.If you can run a raid, you can run a meeting. If you can grind a speedrun, you can grind a certification. Pick the skill you are proudest of and use it once, this week, somewhere it has a witness.
  3. Find one crew that meets in person.A gym, a kitchen, a volunteer shift, a rec league, a trade. It will look boring from the outside. Go three times before you decide.
  4. Keep the code you admire and write it down as your own.Keep your word. Do not talk about people behind their backs. Show up. Three or four lines, on paper, yours. Then keep it in the rooms where it costs you something.
  5. One night a week, the console is off by midnight.No other rule. Not fewer hours, not a detox, not a lecture. One night, one clock, and notice what comes in when it is quiet.

Things worth believing on the way

  • Wanting a crew that keeps its word is not a flaw.
  • Loyalty is a virtue. It deserves a worthy object.
  • The real ones die for men who would not cross the street for them.
  • Respect that has to be taken was never yours.
  • The hours you put into a game were practice in showing up.
  • You can be feared or you can be trusted. Only one of them lets you sleep.
  • The name that matters is the one people say when you are not in the room.

What the stories are really about

It was never the crime.

Every one of these stories is about a man who wants to matter to somebody, and about a world that promises to make that happen fast. The crew in the film keeps its word when nobody else in your life does, and the code gives you a rule to live by when the real world has stopped handing them out. The respect is earned in scenes instead of years, and you get to watch someone who started with nothing become a person other people stand up for. That is not a taste for crime; it is a hunger for loyalty, competence and a name, dressed in the only costume that made it look serious. The stories grip because the hunger is real, and they end the way they do because the world they are set in cannot actually feed it.
