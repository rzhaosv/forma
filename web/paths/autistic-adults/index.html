No. 10 · Coming into your own

Working out the rules late.

Notes on masking, loud rooms, the same breakfast, and friends who don't need translating.

Written plainly for autistic adults, including anyone who got the manual at thirty-four or hasn't yet. Nothing here is about curing you or making you easier to have around. It's about the cost of putting a face on all day, doing less of that where you can, and the routines, rooms and friendships that don't need you to translate yourself first. The mask comes off one room at a time.

Someone kept the light on while you worked out the rules. It still counts.

Noted, from someone who masks

Masking is running two conversations at once: the one everyone can hear, and the one where you monitor your face, your volume, your eye contact, whether you laughed in the right place. It works. It's also why an ordinary Tuesday ends with me unable to speak. So I picked two rooms to do less of it in: my sister's kitchen and the Thursday board-games table. Told them plainly, once, that I might not look at them while talking and that it doesn't mean anything. Nobody minded. The tiredness in those two rooms is about half what it was. The friendships there are the realest I've had. I'm not taking the mask off everywhere. Two rooms was the start, and a third is on the list.

Plainly, to you

A room can be too loud, too bright, or smell wrong. That's real information from your body, not an overreaction. You're allowed to leave, and you don't need a good excuse. “I need a few minutes” is a complete sentence. Here's the practical version I use. Before I go anywhere with a crowd, I find the exit and the quietest spot near it: a stairwell, a car, the bit of corridor by the toilets. Then I go in, and I know where I'm going if it gets too much. Stepping out for ten minutes isn't rude. It's how you stay able to come back, and the people worth being there for would rather have you back than have you white-knuckling it by the speakers.

Plainly, to you

The same breakfast, the same route, the same order for the evening. These aren't flaws to grow out of. A routine is a decision you made once so you don't have to make it every day, and it frees up energy for the parts of life that can't be predicted. Keep them. Change them when you choose to, not when someone calls them rigid. What I'd add, from experience: write the routine down somewhere, even the obvious bits. When a bad week knocks it over, a written list means you can put it back in twenty minutes instead of a fortnight. Mine is on the fridge. Porridge, the same mug, the 8:05 train, the same seat if it's free. Nothing clever. It holds the week up.

Noted, from someone told at thirty-four

Found out at thirty-four. First came a long replay of my life with the sound turned up: the friendships that ended and I never knew why, the jobs that were fine until suddenly they weren't, being told I was too blunt, too sensitive, too much. None of that was a character flaw. I'd been doing something hard without the manual. Now I've got part of it. What I did with it: went back to two of those friends and said one plain sentence about what I'd learned. One of them said, that explains a lot, and we've had lunch every month since. The other didn't reply. That's allowed. One out of two is a good return on a sentence. There's a third I'm working up to.

Plainly, to you

Friendship doesn't have to look like frequent calls and busy group plans. It can be one person you message about one shared interest every few weeks. It can be sitting in the same room doing separate things. You're allowed to say “I prefer text” and “I don't do surprises,” and the people who are right for you won't need you to translate yourself first. Here's a small thing that's worked. Keep one message saved that you can copy: “Low on energy today, can we do this by text?” Sending it takes four seconds and it's saved me three friendships I'd otherwise have gone quiet on and lost. People mostly say yes. The ones who keep saying yes are your people; you can tell by how little it costs.

The steady one

Here's where I've landed, a few years after finding out. I was made on purpose, wiring included, before anyone had an opinion about whether I was too much. The parts that notice everything, feel it strongly, and need things a certain way came together at the same time as the parts that are precise, loyal, honest and deep. One design, not a good half and a faulty half. That's not a feeling; it's the floor I stand on. Then I do the ordinary things. Same breakfast. Two rooms with the mask off, a third on the way. Ten minutes in the stairwell when the room's too loud. One saved message. None of it needs anyone's permission, and nothing about me needs translating to be worth knowing. Working it out late still counts as working it out.

What actually works

None of these need a crowd or an explanation. Pick one.

  1. Eat the meal you know you can eat.Texture-safe, same as yesterday. That counts as looking after yourself.
  2. Put one predictable hour in the calendar.Same time, same place, same thing. Defend it like a dentist appointment.
  3. Save one message you can copy.“Low on energy today, can we do this by text?” Four seconds to send.
  4. Find the exit before you go in.The stairwell, the car, the quiet corridor. Ten minutes out is how you come back.
  5. Pick one room to do less masking in.Tell them plainly, once. See who minds. Usually nobody.

The code

  • Loud is information, not an overreaction.
  • “I need a few minutes” is a complete sentence.
  • Same breakfast, same route. Nothing clever; it holds the week up.
  • Find the exit first, then go in.
  • One saved message beats going quiet.
  • Friends who need translating aren't yours yet.
  • Made on purpose, wiring included. Nothing to translate.
