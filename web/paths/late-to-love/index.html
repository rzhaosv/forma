A path · Still on time

Never been in a relationship. Not too late, and nothing to hide.

Late is still on time, and the first relationship that has not happened yet is not a verdict on you. This is for the ones past the age where never having dated feels normal to say: twenty-seven, thirty-three, forty-one, with a therapist as the only person who knows. You are not behind. You are unpracticed, and practice is available, kind, and closer than the apps make it look.

Unpracticed is fixable. Behind was never true.

From someone whose first kiss was last spring

I am thirty-one and my first kiss happened in April, in a car park behind a Thai restaurant, and I want to tell you what it was actually like, because nobody told me. It was not fireworks. It was not the film version where the music swells. Our noses went the wrong way first, and she laughed, and I laughed, and then it was fine. That is the whole report: it was fine. For fifteen years I had built it into a door I had to get through before I was allowed to be a person, and it turned out to be a doorway with nothing behind it but the same evening, continuing. What I felt walking to the bus was not triumph. It was closer to grief, for the sixteen-year-old who had decided this was the exam. She did not know it was my first. I told her three weeks later. She said, huh, and then, that explains why you were so careful, and then she asked what I wanted for dinner. Nobody was grading me. I had been the only examiner in the room the whole time.

From someone who is good at everything else

I have a job people are impressed by. I own my flat. I ran a marathon in the spring, I can hold a handstand, I speak passable Portuguese. I am thirty-four and I have never been anyone’s girlfriend, and on Sunday at brunch, when the conversation went round the table the way it does, the exes, the one who got away, the one who turned out to be married, I did what I always do, which is laugh at the right moments and reach for the water. There is a version of me these women think they know: too busy, too picky, too many standards. I let them think it, because the true sentence has never been said in daylight. Not because it is shameful, exactly. Because there is no shelf for it. There is no brunch story that goes: nobody has ever picked me, I do not know why, and I have stopped being sure it is fixable. What I noticed on Sunday, walking home, was that one of them had gone quiet at the same moments I had. I have started to wonder if the table has two of us at it. Next Sunday, I am going to ask her.

From someone on the eleventh reinstall

Eleven. I counted, because the app tells you when you last logged in and I have a habit of doing the arithmetic on my own failures. Eleven times since I turned twenty-four I have downloaded the thing, spent a Sunday night choosing five photographs that make me look like someone who goes places, written a bio, deleted the bio, written a worse one, and then swiped for a fortnight with a feeling in my chest like sitting an exam I had not revised for. Two matches, usually. One conversation that dies on the third message. Then the delete, which feels like leaving a party early, which feels like relief, which curdles by Tuesday into the certainty that everyone else is still at the party. What I could not see until a friend said it was that I was treating an app as a verdict. I had never had a girlfriend, so I was using the apps to find out whether I was allowed one, and an app cannot answer that. It can only tell you whether your photos are good. The twelfth time, I am writing the bio without a single joke at my own expense. That is the only change. I will let you know.

From someone whose friends assume there was a divorce

At forty-four the assumption does the work for me. New people at a dinner hear that I live alone and their faces arrange themselves into sympathy, and somewhere in the conversation a divorce gets implied, and I do not correct it. It is easier to be a man who lost something than a man who never had it. That is the lie of omission I have been telling since about thirty-five, and it has a cost I did not budget for: every friendship I have made in the last decade stands on a small false floor. My closest friend, a man I would call at three in the morning, thinks there was a woman in my twenties. There was not. There was a bedroom in my mother’s house, a job I was good at, and a decade that went past like scenery from a train. The person who knows is a therapist, on Thursdays, and she keeps asking the same question, which is what I think would happen if I told him. I keep saying I do not know. I do know. He would say, all right, mate, and ask if I wanted another. I am nearly ready to find out if I am right.

From someone who said it on the third date

I decided in advance, which is the only way I could have done it. Third date, if there was one, before dessert, in a sentence I had said to the bathroom mirror enough times that it had stopped sounding like a siren. There was a third date. Pasta, a table by the window, the point in the evening when the questions get closer to the bone. She asked about my longest relationship. I said: this is my first. Not the first that lasted. The first. I am thirty-two. And then, because I had practised this part too, I asked about the dog she kept mentioning. I watched her face for the thing I had been afraid of for fifteen years, the recalculation, the polite retreat. What I got was a pause, then, oh, okay, then a question about why, not unkind, just curious. I gave her the honest short version, which is that it did not happen, and then it had not happened for so long that not happening became the thing I was. She said that made sense. Then she told me about the dog. Seven months now. She says the sentence I was so frightened of was the moment she decided I was safe.

From someone steady

I met her at thirty-eight, at a pottery class I had joined for reasons that had nothing to do with meeting anyone, which I now think is the only way it works. I want to tell you what the years before were actually for, because I spent them believing they were for nothing, a waiting room with bad magazines. They were not. I learned to cook for one without it feeling like a punishment. I learned which of my friends would drive across the country for me, because two of them did. I got good at being on my own in a restaurant, a cinema, a beach, and what that gave me, when she finally arrived, was that I did not need her to be a rescue. I did not mistake the first person who paid me attention for the person. I had nearly done that at twenty-nine, and I had learned the difference between being noticed and being known. She was not my first kiss, but she was the first who stayed, and she says the man she met was not behind anyone. He was a man with a whole life, a spare chair at his table, and no hole in the middle of him she was expected to fill. The years were for building the table.

Small things that are actually yours to do

None of these need a personality transplant. All of them are practice, and practice is the thing you were missing, not worth. Pick one this week.

  1. Say the sentence out loud, alone, once, and notice that you survived it.“I have never been in a relationship.” To the kitchen, to the car, to the bathroom mirror. Not to fix anything. Just so that the first time it leaves your mouth, nobody is watching, and you find out that it is a sentence and not a siren.
  2. One room a week where you are a regular, not a candidate.A pottery class, a climbing wall, a choir, a Tuesday run club, a board-game night. Somewhere people learn your name because you keep turning up, not because you are looking. Being known by a room is the practice. Whoever you meet there is a bonus.
  3. One honest profile, with no jokes at your own expense.No “socially awkward, sorry in advance.” No “my mum thinks I am a catch.” Five photographs of things you actually do, three sentences about what you like, one about what you are looking for. Write it the way you would describe a friend you respect.
  4. Tell one friend the true version, and let them stop guessing.They have a theory about you already. Picky, busy, private, a divorce nobody mentions. Replace it with the fact, in one sentence, over a drink. Most people, told the truth, say “oh” and then something kind. You get a friend who knows you, and they get to stop tiptoeing.
  5. Pick a first meet where the plan is written down and the exit is allowed.A board-game table, a museum in its first quiet hour, a walk with tea at the end. Somewhere you can be side by side instead of face to face, and where “I am off, this was nice” is a normal sentence. If you want that built in, Plainly is the Forma app that writes the noise level and the exit plan on every invitation.
  6. When it comes up on a date, say it plainly and change the subject.“This is my first. I am thirty-three.” Then ask about their dog. It is a fact, not a confession, and the way you say it is the way they will hear it. The right person will be curious, not alarmed. The wrong person has just saved you both some time.

Things worth believing on the way

  • You are not behind. You are unpracticed, and practice is available.
  • Nobody worth having is counting your exes.
  • The first person who matters will not be grading you.
  • Wanting this is not desperate. It is human.
  • You do not owe anyone the story before the second coffee.
  • Being chosen late is still being chosen.
  • You were worth choosing before anyone did.

What the years were actually for

  • You learned to be alone without being lonely. Mostly.
  • You know what you like, because nobody was choosing for you.
  • You will not mistake attention for love. You have seen the difference from outside.
  • You have friendships that took work, and you know what work looks like.
  • You have watched other people’s first tries, and you learned from the ones that went wrong.
  • You have a life someone can join, not a hole someone has to fill.
