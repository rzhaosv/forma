Love and closeness

Putting yourself out there

For anyone still waiting on the first one, at thirty-three or forty-one.

Everyone assumes you had a first. You didn’t, and you’re past the age where it’s easy to say so. That’s the whole situation, and it isn’t a verdict. You are not behind. You’re unpractised, and practice is available: rooms you turn up to, a bio with no jokes at your own expense, a sentence said plainly over pasta. In no rush. Playing it long.

It is not over. Unpractised is a season, not a sentence.

From someone whose first date was in April

Thirty-one, and my first proper date was in April, a Thai place with a car park out the back. I’d like to report what it was actually like, because nobody does. It was fine. Not fireworks, not the film. I ordered wrong, she laughed, I laughed, and the evening carried on being an evening. For fifteen years I’d made it a door I had to get through before I was allowed to be a person. It was a doorway with the same Tuesday on the other side. I told her three weeks later that it had been my first. She said, huh, that explains why you were so careful, and then asked what I fancied for dinner. Nobody was marking it. I’d been the only examiner in the room.

From someone who’s good at everything else

I’ve a job people are impressed by, a flat with my name on the deeds, a marathon in the spring and passable Portuguese. Thirty-four, and never been anyone’s girlfriend. Sunday brunch, the conversation went round the table the way it does, the exes, the one who got away, and I did what I always do: laughed in the right places and reached for the water. They’ve a theory about me. Too busy, too picky. I’ve let them keep it. What I noticed on Sunday, walking home, was that one of them had gone quiet at exactly the moments I had. I rang her on Tuesday. The table has two of us at it, it turns out. We’re doing the Thursday run club together from next week. Neither of us is looking. Both of us are turning up.

From someone on the twelfth go

Eleven reinstalls. I counted, because the app tells you when you last logged in. Eleven times since twenty-four: a Sunday night choosing five photos that make me look like someone who goes places, a bio, a worse bio, a fortnight of swiping, two matches, one conversation that dies on the third message, delete. What I couldn’t see until a mate said it was that I was treating an app as a verdict. I’d never had a girlfriend, so I was using it to find out whether I was allowed one. An app can’t answer that. It can tell you whether your photos are any good. The twelfth go, I wrote the bio with no jokes at my own expense. That’s the only change. Two replies. Coffee on Saturday, a place with a garden. I’ll let you know.

From someone whose mates assumed a divorce

At forty-four the assumption does the work for me. New people hear I live alone, their faces go sympathetic, and somewhere in the conversation a divorce gets implied. I’ve never corrected it. Easier to be a man who lost something than one who never had it. The cost, which I didn’t budget for: every friendship of the last decade stands on a small false floor. My closest mate, the one I’d ring at three in the morning, thinks there was a woman in my twenties. There was a job I was good at and a decade that went past like scenery. Last Thursday, second pint, I gave him the plain version in one sentence. He said, all right, mate, and asked if I wanted another. Then he asked a real question. The floor holds.

From someone who said it on the third date

I decided in advance. Third date, before dessert, in a sentence I’d said to the bathroom mirror enough times that it had stopped sounding like an alarm. There was a third date. Pasta, a table by the window, the point in the evening when the questions get closer. She asked about my longest relationship. I said: this is my first. I’m thirty-two. Then, because I’d practised this part too, I asked about the dog she kept mentioning. I watched for the recalculation. What I got was a pause, then, oh, okay, then a question about why, not unkind, just curious. I gave her the short version. She said that made sense and told me about the dog. Seven months now. She says that sentence was the moment she decided I was straight with her.

From someone steady

I met her at thirty-eight, at a pottery class I’d joined for reasons that had nothing to do with meeting anyone. The years before, I’d believed, were a waiting room with bad magazines. They weren’t. I learned to cook for one without it feeling like a punishment. I found out which two friends would drive across the country, because they did. I got good at a restaurant, a cinema, a beach on my own, so that when she arrived I didn’t need her to be a rescue, and didn’t mistake being noticed for being known. She says the man she met wasn’t behind anyone. He was a man with a whole life and a spare chair at his table. The years were for building the table. I’d been kept company the whole time, by more than I noticed.

What actually works

None of these need a personality transplant. All of them are practice, and practice was the thing missing, not worth. Pick one this week.

  1. Be a regular somewhere, not a candidate.A pottery class, a climbing wall, a Tuesday run club, a board-game night. People learn your name because you keep turning up, not because you’re looking. Whoever you meet there is a bonus.
  2. Write the bio the way you’d describe a mate you respect.No “socially awkward, sorry in advance.” Five photos of things you actually do, three sentences about what you like, one about what you’re after.
  3. Give one friend the short version, over a pint.They’ve a theory already: picky, busy, a divorce nobody mentions. Replace it with the fact, in one sentence. Most people say “oh,” then something kind, then ask if you want another.
  4. Pick a first meet with the plan written down and the exit allowed.A board-game table, a museum in its first quiet hour, a walk with tea at the end. Side by side beats face to face. If you want that built in, Plainly writes the noise level and the exit plan on every invitation.
  5. When it comes up, say it plainly and ask about their dog.“This is my first. I’m thirty-three.” A fact, not a confession, and the way you say it is the way they’ll hear it. The right person is curious. The wrong one has just saved you both some time.

The code

  • Not behind. Unpractised. Practice is available.
  • Nobody worth having is counting.
  • Be a regular before you’re a candidate.
  • There’s more than one of everything.
  • No jokes at your own expense, in the bio or anywhere.
  • Chosen late is still chosen.
  • You were worth choosing before anyone did. Given, not earned.

What the years were for

  • You learned to be on your own without minding it. Mostly.
  • You know what you like, because nobody was choosing for you.
  • You won’t mistake attention for the real thing. You’ve watched the difference from outside.
  • You’ve got friendships that took work, and you know what the work looks like.
  • You’ve watched other people’s first tries and learned from the ones that went wrong.
  • You’ve a life someone can join, not a gap someone has to fill.
