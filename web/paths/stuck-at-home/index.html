Back on your feet

Getting out of the house.

For anyone who kept their head down at home longer than they meant to.

You went inside for a reason, and the room got comfortable, then small. This is for anyone who has kept their head down at home longer than they meant to. It is a slow, ordinary way of getting out again: the blinds, the doorway, the end of the street. Nothing clever, no big brave gesture. The first hundred yards, on a Tuesday, and someone glad to see you.

The door is lighter than it looks. Start with the blinds.

Start with the blindsRead the notes

What actually works

Pick one. Not the list, one. Come back tomorrow, or next week. Nothing here counts days and nothing resets.

  1. Open the blinds, then a window.Just that. Let the room find out what time it is. Outside air, without going outside.
  2. Stand in the doorway for a minute.Door open, feet inside. Go back in whenever you like. Standing there counts.
  3. Shoes on, then the end of the street.Shoes on the couch is a real step. The end of the street and back is the next one. Then rest; rest is part of it.
  4. Text one person one line."Hey, thinking of you." Nothing else is required. Let them be glad.
  5. Same walk, same time, three days running.The mailbox, the corner, the shop for milk you do not need. Enough times that it stops being a thing.

The code

  • The house is a place to live, not a place to wait.
  • Thirty steps to the mailbox is thirty steps.
  • Fear is a bad narrator. Check its facts.
  • Nobody who matters is keeping a tab.
  • Text first. Explain later, or never.
  • The boring stuff works: light, air, a walk, a bedtime.
  • Someone kept the light on. Go and see.

The first hundred yards

Notes from people who were inside a long while, and one from someone steady.

From someone who stopped counting the days

Day one-hundred-and-something. I stopped counting because the number had started to sound like a verdict, and it is not one; it is a number. The blinds stayed down, the delivery driver was the only face I saw, and every night I said tomorrow. What moved first was small. I opened a window. Not the door, a window. The air was cold and smelled like rain, and I stood there for the length of one song and then shut it. That was the whole day and it counted. The next morning I opened it again before the kettle boiled, which is the trick: tie it to something you already do. Window, kettle, window, kettle. Eleven days now. The door is next, and it is not as far from the bed as it looks.

From someone who did the maths

The house was never the problem; the maths was. Every day inside made the outside dearer: more explaining, more catching up, more faces that would ask where I had been. So I stayed, and the bill grew. A mate said something I wrote on the fridge: the people who matter will just be glad, and the rest were never keeping a tab. I did not believe it, so I tested it. One message, one person, eleven words, four days to write. He wrote back in six minutes and asked if I was free Thursday. No questions about the gap. It turned out he had not known whether I wanted to be found. The bill was imaginary. Thursday was real, and I went.

From someone who mistook the dark for rest

I thought I was resting. It turns out lying in the dark at three in the afternoon with the phone six inches from my face is not rest; it is just lying down. Rest is when the weight comes off, even for a minute. For me that was a shower and clean sheets, and it took me a fortnight to get round to both. I sat on the made bed afterwards and felt, for the first time in a long while, like a person who was tired instead of a person who was in a hole. Small distinction, big difference. Now the sheets go on every Sunday, whatever else the week does. It is the one appointment I keep with the house, and it is the first thing on the list that is mine.

From someone who got as far as the gate

The porch, then the step, then the gate. I gave it a week each and did not go further than the plan said, even on good days, because on good days you overreach and then the bad day feels like a fall. Sixty seconds at the gate with a coffee, every morning, seven days. The neighbour waved on day three. I had built a whole story about the neighbour and it turned out to be a wave. By the second week the gate was boring, which is what you are after. Boring means done. Next is the corner shop, two hundred yards, for milk I do not need. Same time every day, enough times that it stops being a thing. It is not clever. It works.

From someone who put seed on the sill

I could not get to the park, so I put seed on the windowsill and let the park come to me. Sparrows, mostly, and one robin who acts like he owns the place. They turn up at half seven whether I have slept or not. They do not stockpile and they do not check the forecast; they show up, and there is enough. I am not saying I have learned anything grand from a robin. I am saying I stand at the window for ten minutes every morning now, and the window is open, and for those ten minutes I am outside in every way that matters except the feet. The feet are next. I bought the seed from the shop on the corner. Walked there. Twice now.

From someone steady

Nobody who has done this thinks you are lazy. The front door gets heavy for reasons, usually several stacked up, and some of them are older than the door. You do not have to lift all of it; you only have to stand near it for a minute, and then you can go back in. Wanting to be out there, with your people, in the light, is a good thing to want. Point it at the mailbox first. Fear is a loud narrator and it lies about scale: it says the corner is a mile off and the neighbour will stare. Then you go, and it is thirty steps, and the neighbour waves. You are allowed to check its facts, one small step at a time. The corner will still be there tomorrow.
