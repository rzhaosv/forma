Political anxiety, exhausted by the news, family fighting about politics, doomscrolling

When the whole country feels like a fight.

For people who are tired of it. The phone that will not stop, the group chat that turned into a courtroom, the parent who watches a different channel, the friend who is no longer a friend. The feeling that everything is on fire and nothing you do changes any of it, and that you must pick a side before you have finished a sentence. This is not a page about who is right. It is a page about getting your life back.

From someone who lost a friend over it

We were friends for nineteen years. It ended in a comment thread, in front of people neither of us knew. I do not even remember which post. I remember typing something I would never have said across a table, and I remember her reply, and I remember waiting for one of us to be the adult, and neither of us was. That was fourteen months ago. She was at my wedding. I still know her mother's phone number by heart. Here is the thing nobody tells you: I did not win. Nobody read that thread and changed their mind. The issue we fought about is still there, exactly the size it was, and she is not. I have started three messages to her and deleted all three. The one I will eventually send does not mention politics at all. It says I miss her, and that I was cruel, and that I would like to hear about her kids.

From someone who stopped reading the news

I quit the news in the spring. Not a detox, not a plan. I just could not open the app one morning and did not open it the next morning either. The guilt arrived on day three. People are suffering and I am making coffee. What kind of person looks away? So I kept a list of what I actually did with the hours: slept, read two books, went to my nephew's game, fixed the fence. And I could not think of a single person the scrolling had ever helped, including me. What I had been calling staying informed was mostly staying frightened. I am back to reading now, once a day, one long piece, from a source that prints corrections. I know less about what happened this hour. I know roughly as much about what is actually going on. And I have the fence.

From someone whose parents watch a different channel

My parents have a channel on in the kitchen from six in the morning until they go to bed. It is not my channel. When I visit I can feel my shoulders go up before I have taken my coat off. For a while I fought it. I brought facts. They brought facts. We would end up standing in the kitchen with our voices raised over a segment none of us had chosen, and then my mother would cry and my father would go out to the garage. Last Thanksgiving I asked if we could turn it off while I was there, and I promised I would not bring any of it up either. They said yes. It was quiet. My dad told me a story about his first job that I had never heard. I still think they are wrong about a great deal. They think the same of me. The channel does not love them. I do. I am trying to act like the one who does.

From someone who works in it

I have worked in politics for a long time, on campaigns and in offices whose names you would recognize. A few things from the inside. Most of what reaches you was designed to reach you. The anger you feel at ten at night is not an accident; someone tested three versions of it. The people who write the sharpest lines about the other side often have lunch with people from the other side, and they are usually fine. The things that actually change lives are boring: a permit, a bus line, a school board vote, a form somebody helped you fill out. Those things rarely trend. If you want to know who is serious, watch who shows up to the meeting nobody films. And if you want to stop feeling like a pawn, stop taking your instructions from the feed. It is not lying to you, exactly. It is selling you a feeling, and the feeling is not yours to carry.

From someone who is scared for real reasons

I am not anxious in the abstract. I have a reason with a name. Someone in my family could be affected directly by what is being decided, and I do not get to log off from that. So I will not pretend a walk fixes it, and I am tired of being told to calm down as if calm were the goal. Here is what I have found instead. Fear that runs all day is not the same as attention, and it does not protect the person I love. Sleep protects them, because a tired person makes worse calls. A lawyer's number on the fridge protects them. Knowing the actual rule, from the actual document, instead of the version that went around the group chat, protects them. I read the real thing now. It is often less terrifying than the summary, sometimes it is worse, and either way I know. Then I close it and make dinner, because they need me for dinner too.

From someone steady

You can care about something without carrying all of it. That sounds too easy until you try to live it, and then it turns out to be the whole job. The country has been through worse stretches than this one, and it did not feel survivable at the time either. People kept marrying, planting, burying, showing up. That was not denial. It was the part that held. Your attention is the most valuable thing you own, and a great many people are competing for it right now, most of whom will never know your name. Give it to the people who do. Give some of it, honestly and on purpose, to the one or two things that are yours to work on, in the place where you live, where a phone call still reaches someone. Give the rest back to your own life. Nobody is keeping score of how upset you were. They will notice whether you were there.

Small things that are actually yours to do

None of these require picking a side. All of them give you back a little ground. Pick one this week.

  1. One source, read all the way through.Instead of ten headlines skimmed. Pick something that prints corrections. Once a day is plenty.
  2. A time the phone goes in a drawer.Same time every night. The news will be the same in the morning, and so will you, only rested.
  3. One conversation where you only ask questions.With the person you argue with most. No rebuttals. See what you learn when you are not busy building your reply.
  4. One local thing you can actually move.A school board agenda, a pothole report, a neighbor who needs a ride. Small, real, and finished.
  5. A week off the outrage feed.Mute the accounts that exist to make you angry. Notice who you are on day five.

Things worth believing on the way

  • You can care about something without carrying all of it.
  • Being informed and being frightened are not the same thing.
  • Nobody has ever been argued out of a belief in a comment thread.
  • The people who love you are worth more than the people who agree with you.
  • Your attention is yours. It is not owed to anyone who shouts.
  • Most of what matters is local, quiet, and does not trend.
  • You are allowed to have a good day while the news is bad.

Where things stand, as of early September 2026

What is actually being argued about.

A plain list, in case the noise has made it hard to tell. Each line names the issue and what the two sides say they want. None of them says who is right. That part is yours.

  • Prices and the midterms. Congressional elections are in November, and polls put the cost of living at the top of voters' concerns; each party says the other's policies are what made things expensive, and each says its own plan would bring prices down.
  • Government funding. Congress passed a stopgap bill this week that funds the government through December 11, so there will be no shutdown before the election; the argument over a full year of spending, including limits on how the executive branch moves money between programs, has been postponed rather than settled.
  • Immigration. Federal deportations are on pace for a record year; supporters say the law is finally being enforced as written, and critics say the methods are sweeping in people who should not be swept in.
  • Iran. The United States and Iran have been in an on-and-off armed conflict since February; a spring ceasefire and a June agreement both broke down, fire was exchanged again at the end of August, and each government says the other failed to keep its commitments while mediators keep trying.
  • Ukraine. Russia's war in Ukraine continues into its fifth year with front lines moving slowly; Russia says a settlement is possible with American and Chinese backing, Ukraine and many European governments say any deal must not reward the invasion, and the United States has said it will ask Europe to repay past military aid.
  • Election maps and rules. Several states redrew their congressional districts mid-decade, each side saying the other started it; courts have reached different conclusions about which maps are legal, and separate cases over mail-in ballot rules have reached the Supreme Court.
  • AI and jobs. Employers have attributed more than a hundred thousand job cuts this year to artificial intelligence; some argue this is mostly slower hiring and normal churn that retraining can absorb, others argue it needs new rules, and a majority of Americans say they agree with neither party's approach.
  • Universities and speech. The House passed a bill this week that would cut federal student aid from colleges that boycott Israel, and the administration has used funding freezes and investigations to pressure universities; supporters say this enforces civil rights law, and critics say it punishes protected speech.

Written from public reporting in the first days of September 2026. Things move. If a line is out of date, it is because the world moved, not because we changed our mind about the point of this page.
