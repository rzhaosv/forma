Out there

Turning the noise down.

For anyone who still cares, and would like their evenings back from the feed.

You can care about what's going on without carrying all of it, and you can have your evenings back. This is for the ones whose phone doesn't stop, whose group chat turned into a courtroom, whose parents keep a different channel on in the kitchen. Not a page about who's right. A page about attention, sleep, and the one or two things that are actually yours to move.

You're allowed a good evening while the news is bad.

Nineteen years, ended in a comment thread

Nineteen years of friendship, ended in a comment thread in front of people neither of us knew. Fourteen months ago. Noted, and worth noticing what it bought: nothing. Nobody read that thread and changed their mind. The issue is still there, the same size, and she isn't. So I stopped drafting arguments and drafted a message instead. It doesn't mention politics. It says I miss her, I was harsh, and I'd like to hear about her kids. I've written it three times. The fourth one is going out on Sunday, because I still know her mother's phone number by heart, and that seems like the kind of thing you don't throw away over a post I can't even remember.

Off the news since spring

Stopped opening the news app one morning in the spring, and didn't open it the next morning either. No plan, no announcement. By day three the guilt turned up: people are having a hard time and I'm making coffee. So I kept a list of what I actually did with the hours. Slept. Read two books. Went to my nephew's game. Mended the fence. Then I tried to think of one person the scrolling had ever helped, including me, and couldn't. What I'd been calling staying informed was mostly staying frightened. I read again now: once a day, one long piece, from a source that prints corrections. I know less about this hour and about as much about what's actually going on. And the fence is up.

The channel in my parents' kitchen

My parents keep a channel on in the kitchen from six in the morning till bed. Not my channel. For years I walked in, felt my shoulders go up before my coat was off, and brought facts. They brought facts back. It ended the same way every time: voices up over a segment none of us chose, my mother upset, my father out to the garage. Last Thanksgiving I asked if we could switch it off while I was there, and promised I wouldn't bring any of it up either. They said yes. It was quiet. My dad told me a story about his first job I'd never heard. I still think they're wrong about a lot; they think the same of me. The channel doesn't love them. I do. Next visit, same deal.

Notes from someone who works in it

I've worked in politics a long time, on campaigns and in offices you'd recognise. A few things from the inside. Most of what reaches you was built to reach you; the anger you feel at ten at night was tested in three versions first. The people who write the sharpest lines about the other side often have lunch with the other side, and get on fine. The things that change lives are boring: a permit, a bus route, a school board vote, a form somebody helped you fill in. None of it trends. If you want to know who's serious, watch who turns up to the meeting nobody films. And if you want to stop feeling like a pawn, stop taking your instructions from the feed. It isn't lying, exactly. It's selling a feeling.

A reason with a name on it

I'm not worried in the abstract. Someone in my family could be affected by what's being decided, and I don't get to log off from that. So a walk doesn't sort it, and I'm done being told to calm down as if calm were the point. What I've found instead: fear that runs all day isn't the same as attention, and it doesn't protect the person I love. Sleep does, because a tired person makes worse calls. A lawyer's number on the fridge does. Knowing the actual rule, from the actual document, instead of the version that went round the group chat, does. I read the real thing now. Often it's less frightening than the summary; sometimes it's worse; either way I know. Then I close it and make dinner, because they need me for dinner too.

A quiet word from someone further along

You can care about something without carrying all of it. Sounds too easy until you try to live it, and then it turns out to be the whole job. The country's been through worse stretches, and they didn't feel survivable at the time either. People kept marrying, planting, burying, showing up. That wasn't looking away. It was the part that held. Your attention is the most valuable thing you own, and a great many people are competing for it right now, most of whom will never know your name. Give it to the people who do. Give some of it, on purpose, to the one or two local things that are yours to move. Give the rest back to your own evenings. Nobody's keeping score of how upset you were. They'll notice whether you were there.

What actually works

None of these need you to pick a side. All of them give you back a little ground. Pick one this week.

  1. One source, read all the way through.Instead of ten headlines skimmed. Pick one that prints corrections. Once a day is plenty.
  2. The phone in a drawer at the same time every night.The news will be the same in the morning, and so will you, only rested.
  3. One conversation where you only ask questions.With the person you argue with most. No rebuttals. See what you learn when you're not busy building your reply.
  4. One local thing you can actually move.A school board agenda, a pothole report, a neighbour who needs a lift. Small, real, finished.
  5. A week off the outrage accounts.Mute the ones that exist to make you angry. Notice who you are on day five.

The code

  • Care about it without carrying all of it.
  • Informed and frightened are not the same thing.
  • Nobody was ever argued out of anything in a comment thread.
  • The people who love you outrank the people who agree with you.
  • Your attention is yours. Nobody who shouts is owed it.
  • Most of what matters is local, quiet, and doesn't trend.
  • A good day in a bad week is given, not earned. Take it.

Where things stand, as of early September 2026

What is actually being argued about.

A plain list, in case the noise has made it hard to tell what is actually on the table. Each line names the issue and what the two sides say they want. None of them says who is right. That part is yours.

  • Prices and the midterms. Congressional elections are in November, and polls put the cost of living at the top of voters' concerns; each party says the other's policies are what made things expensive, and each says its own plan would bring prices down.
  • Government funding. Congress passed a stopgap bill this week that funds the government through December 11, so there will be no shutdown before the election; the argument over a full year of spending, including limits on how the executive branch moves money between programs, has been postponed rather than settled.
  • Immigration. Federal deportations are on pace for a record year; supporters say the law is finally being enforced as written, and critics say the methods are sweeping in people who should not be swept in.
  • Iran. The United States and Iran have been in an on-and-off armed conflict since February; a spring ceasefire and a June agreement both broke down, fire was exchanged again at the end of August, and each government says the other failed to keep its commitments while mediators keep trying.
  • Ukraine. Russia's war in Ukraine continues into its fifth year with front lines moving slowly; Russia says a settlement is possible with American and Chinese backing, Ukraine and many European governments say any deal must not reward the invasion, and the United States has said it will ask Europe to repay past military aid.
  • Election maps and rules. Several states redrew their congressional districts mid-decade, each side saying the other started it; courts have reached different conclusions about which maps are legal, and separate cases over mail-in ballot rules have reached the Supreme Court.
  • AI and jobs. Employers have attributed more than a hundred thousand job cuts this year to artificial intelligence; some argue this is mostly slower hiring and normal churn that retraining can absorb, others argue it needs new rules, and a majority of Americans say they agree with neither party's approach.
  • Universities and speech. The House passed a bill this week that would cut federal student aid from colleges that boycott Israel, and the administration has used funding freezes and investigations to pressure universities; supporters say this enforces civil rights law, and critics say it punishes protected speech.

Written from public reporting in the first days of September 2026. Things move. If a line is out of date, it is because the world moved, not because we changed our mind about the point of this page.
