The Facebook strategy, in detail.

Five thousand ads, one first line, a two-week kill rule, and a layer of other people’s faces.

Meta shows every active ad to anyone who looks. We looked at 243 of his in June 2026 and read them one at a time: who ran them, what they said, what they showed, how long they lived, and where the click went. This page is that reading, then the clean version you would build from it in thirty days.

Forma has no connection to MEDVi. Every figure here is reported by the New York Times, Forbes, the FDA or Meta’s public ad library, and is linked at the bottom of each page. Nothing in this guidebook is advice to sell medicine.

What the ad library shows

The machine, measured

Meta publishes every active ad on its platforms. In June 2026 we pulled 243 ads under the MEDVi name and the pages that sell it, and read them one at a time. These are the counts. Every figure on this page comes from that sample, not from the company.

At the peak

5,000+

Active ads under the MEDVi name at once, per press reporting. Our June sample is a slice of the tail of that.

In our 243

221

Ads that open with the same first line, word for word: the price, then what is not charged.

One number

$149 × 406

How many times the figure appears across the sample. $166 appears 12 times, $99 seven. Everything else is noise.

Run length

36 days

Median age of a live ad. 165 of the 243 sit between 30 and 59 days. Fourteen made it past 60. One made it to 127.

Launch cadence

166 in May

Ads started in a single month. Sixty more in the first three weeks of June. Volume is bought in waves, not trickles.

Placement

All four

Every ad ran on Facebook, Instagram, Messenger and Threads at once. Nobody hand-picked placements. Advantage+ did.

The account structure

Three layers, and only one of them is his

The single most misread thing about MedVi’s advertising is whose ads they are. Sorting the sample by the page that ran each ad gives three layers.

  1. The brand page: one ad.The page called MEDVi GLP-1 ran exactly one ad in our sample, and it was the oldest thing running: 127 days, started February 18, a benefit card with the headline “Smarter Fat Loss Starts Here” baked into a lifestyle photo. The company’s own name buys almost nothing. It is a landing page with a logo on it.
  2. The persona pages: 222 ads.Two pages named after people carried 222 of the 243 ads, and one of those names carried 203. This is the layer the press and the state attorneys general wrote to Meta about: fictitious people, sometimes with medical titles, running the volume. One detail from the pull matters more than the rest. 154 of those ads showed trimrx.com as the display link, and only 19 showed a MedVi domain. The persona layer is not MedVi’s page wearing a mask. It is an affiliate publisher that sells whichever telehealth offer pays that week, MedVi and its competitor both, from the same face.
  3. The “wellness center” pages: 20 ads.Wellness Access Center, Active Wellness Center, Modern Wellness. Generic names, their own creative, $99 and $166 hooks, tracking links into glp1.medvi.org. These are affiliates paid per patient delivered. They write their own copy and take their own risk, which is precisely why he lets them.

He does not run five thousand ads. He runs one, and pays a layer of other people’s pages to run the other 4,999. The volume is real. The liability is rented.

The copy system

Four angles, one skeleton

Read enough of the ads and the variety collapses. There are four angles, and every one of them is the same five lines in a different order.

  1. The price line. 221 of 243.“Tirzepatide available for ONLY $149/month. No memberships or hidden fees.” That is the whole opener. Price in the first six words, then the two things that are not charged. The headline underneath repeats it: “$149 a Month Tirzepatide Now Available!” ran as the headline on 162 ads. He does not test headlines. He found one.
  2. The scam teardown. The June workhorse.The long-form angle the brand runs in its own name. It opens by agreeing with the reader that the category is full of tricks, names the tricks by dollar figure, and then makes the flat price the relief. The oldest six ads on the brand account in June all carried this copy, re-run as fresh ad IDs.
    You’ve seen the ads. “$59/month!” Then you get to checkout and there’s a $99 membership fee. Then a dosage adjustment charge. Then a “clinical platform fee” you definitely didn’t see coming. By the time you’ve done the math, you’re paying three times what the ad said. Medvi doesn’t do that.Excerpt from the primary text of Meta Ad Library ID 1908002806580323, started June 7, 2026. Followed by a five-line checklist of what $149 includes, a member count, and “Qualify in 2 minutes.”
  3. Flat for life. The $166 angle.Same skeleton, different fear. Where the $149 copy attacks hidden fees, the $166 copy attacks the price rise: “even if the price is real today, it quietly goes up next month.” Then the promise: locked in, no increases, ever. It costs $17 more a month and sells certainty instead of cheapness. Both run at the same time to different people.
  4. The confession opener. Six ads.“You’ve tried. Nobody can say you haven’t.” The only angle in the sample that starts with the reader instead of the price. It is rare because it is expensive to write and easy to get wrong under Meta’s health rules, which forbid implying the reader has a condition. It exists because a feed full of price cards needs one ad that sounds like a person.

The skeleton

  • Line one: the number. Price, drug, per month. Never a range, never “from.”
  • Line two: what is removed. Membership fee, hidden fees, insurance, waitlist, waiting room. Each word deletes a reason to wait.
  • Line three: what is included. A checklist, four or five ticks. Provider consult, ongoing support “not a chatbot,” shipped to your door.
  • Line four: the crowd. “100,000+ members.” Then, by June, “500,000+.” The number grows with the company and is never sourced.
  • Line five: the time. “Qualify in 2 minutes” and an arrow. Not buy. Qualify. The ad sells a quiz, and the quiz sells the drug.
  • The button is Learn more. 171 of 243. Never Shop now, never Sign up. It is the lowest-friction button Meta offers and the one its health reviewers like best.
The creative

Still images with the price burned in

Almost nothing in the sample is produced video. The work is done by static images and UGC-style stills, and the price is in the picture, not only in the text underneath it.

  1. The hand and the pen.A hand holding the injector pen, the price overlaid in large type, and a “supplies are limited” strip. This is the affiliate pages’ default and the format that scrolls past most like a product listing.
  2. The vial on the sheets.One vial resting on white bedding, soft light, phone-camera feel, a short text overlay. It reads as something a customer posted, which is the point. This carried the scam-teardown copy on the brand account.
  3. The wall of vials.Dozens of vials in a grid with “Tirzepatide for $166/mo” across them. Abundance as the image. Paired with the flat-for-life copy, it says: we have plenty, the price is not going anywhere.
  4. The benefit card.A branded colour block, a lifestyle photo, the headline set in the image. The brand page’s only ad, and the longest-lived thing in the sample at 127 days. The one format that does not mention a price in the picture is the one that lasted longest under the company’s own name.

The rule underneath the formats: the picture must answer “what is it and what does it cost” before the thumb stops. The text is for the people who stopped.

The cadence

Launch in waves, kill in two weeks, leave the survivors alone

  • Waves. 166 ads started in May, 60 in June, 14 in April, one in February. Ads go live dozens at a time, not one at a time.
  • Two weeks to live. 61 ads in the sample were under 14 days old, and only 3 were between 14 and 29 days. Ads that do not earn their place in the first two weeks are gone before the third. The gap in the histogram is the kill rule, visible from outside.
  • A month is normal. 165 of 243 were 30 to 59 days old. A surviving ad lives about a month and is then replaced by the same copy under a new ad ID, which resets frequency and Meta’s learning without changing what worked.
  • Quarters are rare and carry the account. Fourteen ads past 60 days. One past 120. Those few do the volume; the hundreds behind them are the search for the next one.
  • Same copy, fresh IDs. The June 7 scam-teardown ran as six separate ads. The June 15 flat-for-life ran as seven. He does not write new copy when a winner tires. He re-uploads it.
After the click

The ad sells a quiz

Every link in the sample lands on a tracking domain, glp1.medvi.org or glp.medvi.org for the house ads, a partner domain for the rest. The page after the click is not a store. It is three questions, a fit verdict, the price, and a handoff to the clinical partner’s intake with the plan already chosen. “Qualify in 2 minutes” is not a slogan. It is a description of the funnel, and it is why the button says Learn more. The purchase happens two pages later on a form that a licensed partner built and that already passed review.

This is the reason the pixel event to optimise is the quiz completion, not the purchase. The purchase fires on someone else’s domain, days later, after a clinician has looked. Optimise on the thing you can see.

What to copy, what to leave

The clean version of the same machine

Half of this page is a compliance case study. Here is the split.

  1. Copy: price first, in the picture and the first line.Nothing about that is regulated. It is the single highest-leverage habit in the sample.
  2. Copy: the five-line skeleton and the Learn more button.Number, removed, included, crowd, time. Use a member count only if you can source it.
  3. Copy: waves, the two-week kill, the fresh-ID re-upload.This is the operating rhythm. It costs discipline, not money.
  4. Copy: still images, UGC feel, the quiz as the landing.All of it builds in an afternoon with the tools on page two.
  5. Leave: the persona pages.Fictitious people with medical titles are the exact thing in the attorneys general letter to Meta and in the FDA’s view of the site. Run your ads under your brand page with your own face, your co-founder’s face, or a customer who signed a release. The lift from a face is real. The lift from a fake face is a loan.
  6. Leave: the word compounded, and anything that sounds like approval.“Prescription GLP-1 care” is the phrase. Meta’s prescription drug policy lets only LegitScript-certified advertisers with written permission name the drugs at all, in the United States, Canada and New Zealand, to adults. Get the certificate and the permission from page two before a single ad is built.
  7. Leave: before-and-after, body close-ups, and “you” with a condition attached.Meta’s personal health and appearance rules reject all three, and a rejected health ad on a young account is how accounts get restricted. The confession opener works because it names effort, not a body.
  8. Leave: the unsourced crowd number.“500,000+ members” with no source is a claim a regulator can ask you to substantiate. Use a real number, or use none.
Build sheet

Your first thirty days on Meta

Assumes the certificate, the written permission and the warmed-up account from page two. If you do not have all three, you are not on day one yet.

  1. Structure.One campaign, Sales objective, campaign budget on, $50 a day. Three ad sets, one per angle: price line, scam teardown, flat for life. Advantage+ audience, United States, 18 and over, all placements. Do not narrow by interest. His ads did not, and the price line is the targeting.
  2. Events.Pixel on the quiz, Lead fires on the verdict page, Purchase fires by server event from the partner’s webhook if they offer one. Optimise every ad set for Lead. Accept the Custom Audience terms now so the retargeting set can exist later.
  3. Creative, day one.Twenty ads. Four images, the hand and pen, the vial, the wall, the benefit card, each with your price set in the image. Five copies across the three angles. Every copy carries the five-line skeleton. Button: Learn more.
  4. The kill rule.Any ad past eight dollars of spend with no Lead is paused. Any ad past forty dollars with a cost per Lead more than double the set’s best is paused. Check once a day, at the same hour. Do not touch anything else.
  5. Day fourteen.Whatever is still running has earned its place. Re-upload the two best as fresh ads with no changes. Add a retargeting set at $10 a day: site visitors 180 days, quiz starters who did not finish, the flat-for-life copy.
  6. Scaling.Raise the campaign budget 20 percent every three days while cost per Lead holds. Stop raising the day it breaks. Never double.
  7. Run it from the terminal, not the dashboard.Everything on this sheet, the campaign, the three sets, the twenty ads, the daily kill pass and the 20 percent raises, can be done by Claude through Pipeboard’s Meta Ads MCP, a Meta Business Partner connector with a free plan. You describe the rule once; it checks the numbers at the same hour every day and pauses what failed. The dashboard is for looking. The terminal is for the discipline the cadence needs.
  8. Day thirty.You will have one or two ads that are a month old and paying. Those are the account. Launch the next wave of twenty behind them, and start paying affiliates per patient only now, on their own pages, with your copy rules in the contract.

The whole strategy fits on one card: say the price in the picture, sell a quiz not a drug, launch twenty, keep two, run your own face. He did four of the five.
